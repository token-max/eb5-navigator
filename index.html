If you have never heard of EB-5

Four minutes. No jargon. Everything after this section assumes you have read it.

EB-5 is a US green card you buy with an investment instead of a job. No employer sponsors you, no lottery decides it, and nobody can take it away when you change jobs. What it costs is $800,000, roughly $70,000 in fees, and a paper trail proving every rupee of it is legally yours.

Almost nobody builds a business themselves. You put the money into a pooled fund run by a government-licensed sponsor — usually a hotel, a housing development or a data centre — alongside fifty or so other investors. The jobs that project creates are counted as yours. Your money is lent to the developer and comes back years later, when their loan matures.

Two separate things have to go right, and this is the single most important idea on this page. The government has to approve you — that turns almost entirely on where your money came from. The project has to give the money back — that turns entirely on the deal you signed, and your immigration status has no effect on it whatsoever. People conflate these two and lose the second one while celebrating the first.

For many people this, not the green card, is the moment life changes.

The constraint

Three dates, two of which are not what you think

One of these is a genuine wall. One is insurance you are choosing to go without. One is a year away and is the reason the first one exists at all.

Three dates get talked about. Only one of them costs you real money — 1 January 2027, when the price rises from $800,000 to $900,000. The famous 30 September date buys insurance against a rule change that may never come.

What you give up by not filing in September

8 U.S.C. §1153(b)(5)(S) is lapse insurance. It obliges USCIS to keep processing petitions filed on or before 30 September 2026 even if Congress lets the regional-centre programme expire at its 30 September 2027 sunset, and forbids denying them on that ground.

"…shall continue processing petitions … filed on or before September 30, 2026; …may not deny a petition described in clause (i) based on the expiration of such legislation; and …may not suspend or terminate the allocation of visas to the beneficiaries of approved petitions." 8 U.S.C. §1153(b)(5)(S), added by RIA §108, Pub. L. 117-103 Div. BB

This is a real, uninsured risk and it is the strongest argument for rushing. No bill in the 119th Congress reauthorises the 2027 sunset, FY2027 appropriations are stalled, and the last lapse — 30 June 2021 to 15 March 2022 — ran 8½ months, during which USCIS suspended adjudication of regional-centre petitions. At 12–18 months of rural processing, a November filing would still be pending on that date.

Why it loses anyway

A denied September petition is not grandfathered either. §(S) protects petitions, not attempts. If you file thin to beat the date and it fails, you have bought no insurance, lost the fees, lost the year, and still have to refile. The insurance is only worth something if the petition underneath it is approvable.

The thing that makes the insurance worth more than I first said

There is now a competing programme — announced by executive order, with a registration portal and its own form, priced at $1 million for an individual and $1 million more per dependent. On price alone it is worse than EB-5 for you: more money, no returnable investment. That is not the reason it matters.

It matters because it gives the administration something else to sell. If the policy preference shifts to a programme that raises money directly, the political will to reauthorise the regional-centre programme in 2027 weakens — and the §(S) insurance you forgo by filing in November is precisely insurance against that.

What practitioners are actually saying about it, mid-2026

It has not been enacted by Congress and has no regulations — one attorney describes lawsuits already being contemplated over whether visa numbers can lawfully be directed to it at all. So it is not a path you can take instead, and not a settled threat either.

But the industry is pricing the risk. One practitioner said plainly in June that he is less optimistic than his colleague because the new programme "is going to be the thing that they're going to try and sell", and that his firm was revising its service contracts to give no assurances at all for any filing after 1 October. When lawyers change their contracts, that is a real signal about how they price the risk — not commentary.

Another expects serious negotiation on EB-5's future to begin only after 30 September 2026, and flags that the new programme's stricter source-of-funds tracing may become the EB-5 standard too.

This does not flip the recommendation. A petition denied for a thin source-of-funds file buys no insurance at all, and yours cannot be made strong by 30 September. But it does mean the insurance you are giving up is worth more than a simple reading of the sunset suggests — so treat 2027 reauthorisation as genuinely uncertain rather than likely, and get the petition filed as early in November as the evidence allows. Every week of delay past that is now costing you something real.

The strongest argument for filing by 30 September — and why it still fails here

Grandfathering is binary. Filing in October forfeits it with certainty; filing by 30 September forfeits it only if the petition is denied. On that reading a rushed September filing looks like a free option, and several practitioners argue exactly that.

It does not work for your file, and the reason is specific. The partial-funding route — wire what you have, complete the rest later — requires source-of-funds documentation for the full $800,000 filed with the petition. It buys time on moving money, not on documenting it, and documenting three donors is your only real constraint. And the risk window is narrower than it looks — filing closes it

Your priority date is the day your petition is properly filed, fixed then and never moved by a cutoff imposed afterwards. A later retrogression does not push you back in the queue; it only gates two things — final action, and whether you may file the adjustment application concurrently.

So the exposure is not "rural retrogresses at some point in the next few years." It is precisely: does rural retrogress in the weeks between now and the day you file? Every source above says that is unlikely, and the October bulletin will tell you before you commit. Once your petition is in, retrogression costs you time at the back end, not your place in line.

The contagion rule — why the weakest donor file sets the price for all of them

The most common defect across all 469 decisions is not a missing document. It is inconsistency — evidence that contradicts other evidence — at 30.5%, ahead of every specific omission. And the Policy Manual makes one bad file everyone's problem: "Doubt cast on any aspect of the evidence as submitted may lead to a reevaluation of the reliability and sufficiency of the remaining evidence." Worse, it forecloses the obvious response: "Attempts to explain or reconcile the conflicting accounts, absent competent objective evidence pointing to where the truth, in fact, lies, will not suffice."

In a four-donor package the weakest file does not fail alone. It licenses the officer to re-open the other three. This is the strongest argument on the page for bringing fewer donors and for filing only when every file is complete.

One boundary in your favour

The records requirement reaches one hop. When USCIS demanded tax returns for a donor's brothers, going back to a company founded in 1975, the appeals body rejected it: the statute identifies required information "for the immigrant investor, donors, and non-bank lenders" — and stops there. Tracing the path of the money still goes deeper, but the records list ends at your donors.

Every donor is a whole second petition

This is the crux, and it is arithmetic rather than judgement. The rule does not ask donors for a note confirming the gift. It asks them for everything it asks of you.

Be precise about what is actually slow

The seven years of Indian returns are not the bottleneck — ITR-V and Form 26AS download from the income-tax portal in minutes. The bottleneck is tracing each donor's origin of wealth back to lawful savings: property deeds, business books, demat and mutual-fund histories, and a narrative a sceptical officer will accept. That is what practitioners price at four to six weeks per single donor, it is where appeal decisions actually turn, and it has to happen three or four times over.

The NRO route solves one problem and creates another

Routing gifts through your own NRO account is right, and it defeats the single most common gift defect on the record: money moving through a third party. In the failed cases the parents' money went to a business partner or a currency agent who converted and forwarded it, and the officer could not establish the petitioner was ever the intended recipient. Your money lands in your own account, in your own name. Say so explicitly in the source-of-funds memo — it is a structural advantage most petitions do not have.

But the same account is a commingling point. Three or four donors' rupees land in one balance, mix, and leave as dollars. That is exactly the shape of a documented failure in which the officer found the invested dollars "derived from a different collection of funds" and the gifted money never actually left the origin country. Commingling is what breaks a trace.

Asked to name the single biggest source-of-funds headache, a WR Immigration partner answered commingling: “when you have credits that go into an account and now that money is all commingled, and USCIS is going to ask, well, where did all of these different credits come from?”

Six rules that close it — decide these before a rupee moves

1 · Open a dedicated NRO account used for nothing else. Near-zero opening balance, no salary, rent, dividend or interest credits. Every unrelated credit becomes a question you have to answer.

2 · One donor, one credit, one date. No donor splits a gift across transfers unless the annual limit forces it — and where it does, each tranche cites its own gift deed in the narration.

3 · Make the bank narration carry the donor's name on every inbound credit, and keep the transfer advice showing the debiting account.

4 · Remit outward only once every gift has landed, ideally as a single wire whose amount equals the sum of the credits. One outward wire that ties arithmetically to the inbound credits is the cleanest possible answer to "is this the same money?"

5 · No donor's money touches any account but their own and this one. No relative collecting on another's behalf, no hop through an NRE account, no third-party cheque.

6 · Reconcile on one page. Donor → amount → gift-deed date → debiting account → credit date → the outward wire it funded. Sums tie. That single page is worth more than any number of affidavits.

What each gift deed has to say on its face

Cheap to get right and expensive to get wrong — a deed that reads like a loan disqualifies the capital outright, because the rules exclude any debt arrangement from what counts as investing.

  • The gift is unconditional, irrevocable and absolute; title passes on transfer.
  • No repayment, no interest, no schedule, no security interest, no lien — and no expectation of any of them.
  • The donor retains no interest of any kind in the funds, in the enterprise, or in any return.
  • The funds are the donor's own, and the donor is sole legal and beneficial owner. One petition failed precisely because the donor was holding money repayable to someone else.
  • The relationship, stated in the terms of the company-law definition the RBI rule incorporates — which excludes grandparents, uncles, aunts and cousins.
  • The exact amount, currency, date and debiting account.
  • A recital that the gift is exempt in your hands as a gift from a relative under Indian tax law — contemporaneous corroboration, from a document nobody wrote for USCIS, that Indian law also treats it as a genuine gift.
  • Executed and dated before the transfer it describes. A gift dated after the money moved has failed on the record. Notarise, and register where the state allows it.

No fixed cutoff, and the standard is quoted almost verbatim across dozens of decisions: bank letters and deposit statements are not enough, and the record must trace the path of the funds back to a lawful source. But there are two bounds worth knowing, and one of them was set this year.

Three donors, not more — and the reason is not what people assume

Across 469 published appeal decisions, donor count is never itself the stated problem. Fifty-seven discuss gifts substantively and nine involve multiple donors; in none is the number the defect. The problems are always per-donor documentation, intermediaries, ownership, or chronology. The one statutory hook for a structuring concern is narrow and aimed at one person's money routed through nominees to disguise its origin — not several relatives each genuinely giving their own.

The multiplication is still not harmless, for two reasons the record does support. Each donor is an independent chance to fail — and under the rule that inconsistent evidence in one part of a petition undermines the rest, a failure in one file re-opens the others. If each file were independently 90% likely to be clean, three donors is 73% and five is 59%. That arithmetic is illustrative rather than measured, but the direction follows from a real rule. And each donor adds calendar time on the critical path, which is the constraint that decides your filing date.

Take three. Take a fourth only if a donor's annual allowance is partly spent and the arithmetic forces it. Never add a donor for social reasons.

The one lever entirely in your hands

Every additional dollar you fund from US-earned savings deletes an entire evidentiary chain — no donor tax returns, no gift deed, no CA certificate, no tracing someone else's wealth, no RBI limit, no Form 15CA/15CB, no IRS Form 3520 exposure. What the officer sees instead is your W-2s, your 1040s and your bank statements. Getting from under $300,000 to $550,000 takes you from four donors to two and halves the slowest work in the entire petition.

That trade has a price and it is computed further down: at a 70% embedded gain you liquidate about $405,000 of portfolio to deliver $300,000. Expensive — and still very likely the cheapest risk reduction available to you, because it is buying petition safety in the one place the petition is most likely to fail.

If it fails, the tax system returns almost nothing

  • As a salaried engineer with no operational role you are a passive limited partner by definition. Losses suspend and cannot offset your salary until you dispose of the entire interest.
  • Capital losses offset $3,000 of ordinary income a year. An $800,000 capital loss takes roughly 267 years to absorb without other capital gains.
  • Whether the loss is ordinary or capital is not your choice — it turns on the enterprise's debt structure, and the common loan model pushes toward the worse treatment.

So the realistic recovery on a failed $800,000 is whatever fraction the collateral yields, plus a deduction worth a few thousand dollars a year. Price it that way.

Escrow — ask for a ladder, not the top rung

I have revised this three times as the evidence came in, and this is where it lands. The Policy Manual permits escrow held until you actually have status — release contingent only on approval of the petition and of your adjustment application. It is legally available. The statute excludes from qualifying capital anything invested "with a guaranteed rate of return", and a contractual right to repayment is excluded even where it is contingent on the enterprise's success. A deal that promises your money back is not a valid EB-5 investment.

what a real waiver looks like

It names the actual relationship ("this firm represents Regional Centre X as issuer's counsel on this offering and N others"), states concretely how that could disadvantage you, discloses the exact compensation flowing to the firm and any intermediary, and is signed before engagement. Boilerplate is not consent.

What survives a regional-centre failure — less alarming than the numbers suggest

Terminations reportedly ran 14 in 2024 and 195 in 2025, and that figure is widely read as 195 investors each facing a second $800,000. The statute does not say that. If your regional centre is terminated, the first remedy Congress wrote is that your enterprise associates with a different approved centre — an act of the enterprise, requiring no new money from you, and the statute expressly waives the geographic-boundary objection to make it easy. A fresh investment is the fallback, needed only if no centre will adopt it.

The expensive case is different and narrower: debarment of the enterprise or the project company itself — a finding of misconduct at the project, not a paperwork failure at the centre. Even then the additional capital is capped at what is "solely to the extent necessary to satisfy remaining job creation requirements". Where the jobs are already created, that can be nothing.

One update, from the July 2026 proposed rule. DHS proposes to treat termination of an enterprise or project company as equivalent to debarment. It says so to make sure the good-faith-investor protections apply rather than leaving investors with nothing — genuinely protective. But it also means a terminated enterprise, not just a debarred one, lands you in the branch that can require additional capital. The distinction that matters is centre versus enterprise: your regional centre failing is survivable; your enterprise failing is the costly one.

Which flips a diligence priority: the sponsor's own conduct matters more than the regional centre's brand. And if a second investment ever is required, the binding constraint is not the money — it is the 180 days you get to do it in. Assembling three or four fresh Indian donor files in 180 days, under duress, with a terminated project, is not realistic. This is a risk to structure against, not one to react to.

Throughout: your petition, your priority date and your derivatives' protection from ageing out all survive. What does not survive is the capital.

Practicalities

California, your H-1B, and who to hire

Living in California changes nothing about your place in the queue; US immigration has no state quotas. What matters here is your H-1B running out, the California tax bill when you sell shares, and hiring a lawyer who works for you rather than for the project.

There are no state quotas

None exist anywhere in US immigration law. Per-country limits key on country of birth, not residence. California is neutral for your queue position. It matters in exactly one interesting way, which is that your project will be somewhere else.

Two squares. 842,317 of California's 39,431,263 people live outside any metropolitan statistical area — 2.1%. The 20,000-person town carve-out removes more. 21 of 58 counties. Most of the state's land. 2.1% of its people. Your rural project will be in another state, and that is fine — where you live and where you invest are unrelated.

The rest of California's effect is small. Employment-based adjustment applications go to a service centre, not your local field office. And California taxes the partnership income the deal issues you — but on an offering distributing well under one percent, that is a few hundred dollars a year, not a consideration. Expect a nonresident return in the project's state, with a California credit.

The one thing that makes a denial survivable

“If you’re outside the US, a denial — your funds are still safe and you can file a new petition. If you’re inside the US on an H-1B and you’ve relied on the adjustment of status concurrent filing, there are other immigration implications.” Investors who kept H-1B status could file a fresh I-526E and a fresh I-485. Those who had let it lapse had to leave.

He prices the alternative too: an appeal “can take up to a year”, landing you in 2027 at $900,000 and nearer the sunset — so counsel are advising a clean re-file over an appeal, trading roughly twelve months of priority date for a new work permit and travel document. Keeping the H-1B alive is what turns a denial from a catastrophe into a delay.

The one check nobody did for you — do it yourself, it takes ten minutes

Federal discipline lists for immigration practitioners were searched in full for every name here: no matches, current or previous. Broad enforcement searches also came back clean for all of them. But that is not a clean bill of health — the authoritative source is each state's own bar database, and those run as interactive applications that cannot be searched programmatically.

Before you sign, look up each named lawyer on their state bar site — California, New York, Florida, Texas, Pennsylvania, Illinois, New Jersey and Washington all publish free lookups. You want "Active" status and no discipline record. Two minutes per name. Nobody else will do it and it is the only authoritative check.

The seven questions, and why they matter

A lawyer may not accept payment from someone other than you unless you give informed consent and their independent judgement is genuinely unaffected — and may not let whoever recommends or pays them direct that judgement. A bundled "free" attorney means the regional centre picked your lawyer.

  1. Who is paying your fee — only me, or does any part come from the regional centre, developer, agent or broker, directly or indirectly?
  2. Does your firm represent this regional centre, this enterprise, or any affiliated entity — on this project or any other?
  3. If an agent introduced us, is there a written agreement between you, and is it exclusive?
  4. What is the total that agent receives on my $800,000, and where is it disclosed in the project's filings?
  5. Have you or your firm ever taken a position adverse to this regional centre?
  6. Is my engagement letter the same template used by other investors in this deal, drafted by the centre's counsel?
  7. If the project fails, will you bring a claim against the regional centre on my behalf?

Hedging on any of the first four is the answer. "We work closely with them but don't represent them" is a distinction without a difference. And a referral relationship that is non-exclusive in name only does not cure the conflict.

Questions one to four are not hypothetical — the regulator has charged this repeatedly

An immigration lawyer taking an undisclosed per-investor commission from the regional centre they steer their own clients into is a recurring enforcement theory across a full decade, not an isolated scandal. A December 2015 sweep charged six or more immigration attorneys nationally for exactly that. A 2016 order hit a regional centre for being the payer. A 2018 order settled with a regional centre and its chief executive over transaction-based payments to unregistered solicitors across 37 partnerships — $11.6 million. A 2018 case charged a lawyer with secretly owning the regional centre she placed her own clients into, taking more than $10 million in undisclosed commissions from "hundreds" of her own legal clients. A 2023 complaint names an immigration law firm as a co-defendant.

You will not detect this from how someone behaves in a meeting. You detect it by asking who pays them, in writing, and reading the answer.

Two things to fix in the engagement itself

Pay in milestones, not half up front. Investors currently report paying more than half the fee at signature, then losing visibility — "no idea about SOF status, the law firm either replies with generic messages or doesn't reply at all". Split it: retainer, source-of-funds memo delivered, petition filed. Firms are unusually busy right now, and an unstaged fee removes your only leverage over attention. reported by investors

Get a named lawyer, not a firm. Ask who will actually draft the source-of-funds narrative and who answers your email. At two of the firms above, the India experience and the individual-investor practice sit with different people.

What is genuinely unknown

  • The repayment rate for EB-5 capital. No regulator collects it, no rating agency covers the asset class, and every trade source is sponsor-funded. This absence is the most decision-relevant fact on the money side of the page.
  • When the rural set-aside gets a cutoff date. The arithmetic says it must — India alone filed enough rural petitions in half of FY2026 to consume roughly three-quarters of the entire global annual allocation. The timing is exactly what the recommendation turns on and it cannot be resolved from here.
  • Whether the one-year H-1B extension is available on a pending EB-5 petition. The regulation's text appears to allow it; no guidance confirms it. Ask counsel.
  • Whether approval-contingent escrow is actually on offer in the deals worth having, or only in the ones that cannot fill. Ask sponsors directly.
  • How many regional centres are actually approved. The government's own page says 567 as of 11 May 2026; the machine-readable file it links to contains 577. A ten-centre gap in the government's own published data, with no way to tell which is current. It changes nothing you decide, but it is a fair measure of how good the official data is.
  • Whether the named sponsors' project approvals are real. Every claim traces to the sponsor. The government's list of approved centres will not render without a browser, and one project was found with two marketers giving contradictory approval statuses. Ask for the notice; do not accept a description of it.
