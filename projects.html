Could the high-unemployment queue ever be the faster one — and can you take rural speed with a high-unemployment seat?

Short answer: no on both, and the first one is backwards on today’s numbers. ✔ It does not matter for this filing at all. Availability is tested at the moment the package is received, and once an I-485 is on file retrogression cannot remove it. Rural reads C for India today, so a package received in September is safe on a chart that already exists. The whole question is about a re-filing, not this one.

1. Rural is not the crowded one — it is the roomy one, by a factor of two. The reserve is 20% against high unemployment’s 10%, so roughly 1,988 visas a year against 994. And demand runs the same way: USCIS’s own pending adjustment inventory of August 2026 shows 9,193 rural against a 1,988 supply — about 4.6 years already lodged — and 8,967 high-unemployment against a 994 supply, about 9.0 years. The category being proposed as the escape hatch is already twice as oversubscribed as the one being escaped. Both counts are floors: cells under 11 are suppressed. New filings say the same, on the fuller nine-month table below.

2. For the reversal to happen, rural filings would have to more than double relative to high-unemployment filings AND close a 4.4-year gap. That is not impossible over a decade, but it is a bet against both the supply ratio and the current inventory, and rural is also the only one of the two that carries a statutory processing priority. Structurally, rural is the advantaged category and the dual basis is a hedge pointing the wrong way.

✔ A second, independent channel says the same thing — new filings, not the standing queue. USCIS publishes I-526E receipts by category and country. Over the nine months of FY2026 reported so far: rural-only 2,624, an annualised 3,499 a year against a 1,988 supply — 1.76× oversubscribed; high-unemployment-only 1,685, an annualised 2,247 against 994 — 2.26×. High unemployment is 28% more oversubscribed than rural on incoming demand as well as on standing inventory, and both count petitions rather than visas, so each understates the true draw by the size of the families behind them. Two channels, measured differently, pointing the same way.

🔴 And USCIS counts dual-basis petitions as their own category — which sharpens the gap rather than closing it. Its receipts table carries five buckets: rural, high unemployment, “Rural Area and High Unemployment Area”, infrastructure and unreserved. The dual bucket took 421 petitions in nine months and is growing fast — 21 in October to 71 in June, a 3.4× rise — while rural-only filings FELL from 313 to 246 over the same months. India is 200 of those 421, at 47.5% the largest single share. So the agency plainly records the dual basis at the petition stage. What it does not have is a dual visa symbol. A petition USCIS itself files under “rural and high unemployment” must still be charged to exactly one reserve at the end, and nothing published says which, or who chooses. That is a harder fact than silence: the category exists on one side of the process and has no counterpart on the other.

⚠ One reserve genuinely is uncrowded, and neither finalist can use it. Infrastructure took 0 receipts in nine months against roughly 199 visas a year, and the unreserved category took 21 of 4,986 — 0.4%, because for India it reads U. The crowding is not an accident of one category; it is where every filer is being funnelled.

3. On mixing the two — the theory is coherent and the mechanism does not exist. Speed and seat are decided at different stages by different processes: priority processing is a petition-stage adjudication rule that attaches to rural, while the reserve charged is fixed at visa issuance or adjustment by the symbol assigned. So in principle one case could be adjudicated in the rural queue and charged to the other reserve. But there are four regional-centre symbols and they are mutually exclusive — one visa, one symbol, one reserve — and nothing in the statute, the regulation, the proposed rule, all eight Policy Manual chapters, the form instructions or the State Department manual grants anyone a choice between them, or says who picks. Every one of those zeros carries a control that fired. And common sense cuts the same way: USCIS operates a named rural queue and assigns petitions to it. A case sitting in that queue is being treated as rural; expecting it to be charged elsewhere at the end, with no provision saying so, is the kind of assumption that is only tested when it is too late to change.

What the second determination IS worth, stated positively: it is redundancy, not capacity. If a boundary redrawing ever put the site inside a metropolitan area and cost it the rural basis, the high-unemployment determination would still support the $800,000 threshold. That is a real protection and it is the honest version of the benefit. ⚠ It is also the thinner of the two grounds — the project tract is 2.4% unemployment on its own and only qualifies combined with a neighbour at 10.1% — and it expires 6 November 2027, where rural has no clock at all.

E1Being able to stay and workthe detail →

Three things move this and none of them is about visa numbers. Adjustment of status is a discretionary grant, and the memorandum restating that in May 2026 is in force and unenjoined, sets no effective date and no transition rule, and reserves the power to issue guidance for “discrete populations” that has not issued. A court has already used it to excuse a 14-month delay on a concurrent filing. Separately, since 30 October 2025 a work permit tied to a pending I-485 no longer auto-extends while its renewal is pending — the renewal has to be approved before the card expires. And the third is the asymmetry that defines concurrent filing. The two halves of one envelope have completely different remedies if they fail. The petition half carries a full administrative appeal under INA 203(b)(5)(P)(i)(III). The adjustment half carries none: 8 CFR 245.2(a)(5)(ii) says “No appeal lies from the denial of an application by the director”, and the applicant instead “retains the right to renew his or her application in proceedings under 8 CFR part 240”. Part 240 is removal proceedings. That is the appeal forum for the adjustment half. The only other routes are a motion to reopen, or the agency choosing on its own to certify the case for review. ⚠️ And there are two different removal-proceeding postures with opposite burdens — do not let them merge. Renewing a denied I-485 under part 240 means asking an immigration judge for relief, and the applicant carries it. Reviewing a denied I-829 is the reverse, and the regulation says so: 8 CFR 216.6(d)(2) — “No appeal shall lie from this decision; however, the investor may seek review of the decision in removal proceedings. In proceedings, the burden shall rest with USCIS to establish by a preponderance of the evidence that the facts and information in the investor’s petition for removal of conditions are not true.” So the later failure has the better forum. An I-829 denial also terminates residence as of the decision date and issues a notice to appear — which is what puts the investor in the forum where the government has to prove its case. ⚠️ And E1 has a hard outer clock that this page did not carry until now: three years, not “a reasonable time”. The current regulation and the current Form I-829 both still use the open-ended pre-2022 wording — item 5 of the form asks for jobs “expected to be created within a reasonable time” — and that wording is what most commentary still repeats. The statute Congress wrote in 2022 is not open-ended. 8 U.S.C. 1186b(d)(1)(B)(ii) requires jobs not yet created at the I-829 to be created “before the third anniversary of the alien’s lawful admission for permanent residence, provided that such alien’s capital will remain invested during such time”. And 1186b(c)(3)(B)(ii) allows a discretionary one-year extension, then says that absent a further petition “the conditional status shall terminate at the end of such additional year”. The July 2026 proposed rule implements it verbatim at proposed 8 CFR 216.6(g)(3), and for petitions filed on or after 15 March 2022 the proposed text drops the expectation language entirely: proposed 216.6(d)(2)(iii) reads simply “Created at least 10 full-time jobs for qualifying employees”. Read together: the conditional card is not indefinitely renewable while a project catches up. It runs three years from the grant of conditional residence, four at the outside, and then terminates by its own terms. That is the date to hold a project’s job-delivery schedule against — not the I-829 filing window, and not “reasonable”. It cuts both ways: it bounds how long the money has to stay at risk, and it is a deadline a slow build can miss. And here is how often that final stage actually fails, with the one caveat that decides whether the number is any use. Across USCIS’s own quarterly I-829 series for FY2020 Q1 to FY2026 Q2 — all 26 quarters, once the three that USCIS published only as page images and the one it released as a separate CSV are recovered — 1,567 denials in 21,705 completions, 7.22%. By fiscal year the series runs 4.89% (FY2024) → 6.99% (FY2025) → 7.07% (FY2026 to date): the rise happened a year ago and has since flattened, and FY2025 Q4 alone (9.80%) is worse than FY2026 Q2 (7.94%). ⚠️ Do not price one deal off that mean. The quarterly rates run from 1.36% (FY2020 Q1) to 19.02% (FY2023 Q2) — a fourteen-fold spread, and an overdispersion factor of 16.7 against the model where each investor fails independently (chi-square 418 on 25 degrees of freedom). About 55% of that is a fiscal-year level shift, and a residual factor of 9.9 survives inside single years. In plain terms: outcomes cluster by project. A 7% average is made of projects near zero and projects that fail wholesale, so the average describes the programme and says almost nothing about any one offering. USCIS itself labels the series a window rate — some applications approved in a quarter were filed in an earlier one. And no USCIS publication, current or archived, breaks I-829 outcomes out by project, regional centre or new commercial enterprise, so the clustering can be measured in aggregate but not attributed. That is why the per-project questions on this board are the ones that matter and the programme-wide rate is not. One more, and it is 24 days old. Matter of Delcarmen-Lara, 29 I&N Dec. 830 (BIA, 13 August 2026) holds that leaving on advance parole is a “departure” for the unlawful-presence bar at INA 212(a)(9)(B)(i), overruling Matter of Arrabally and Yerrabelly. So once unlawful presence passes 180 days, travelling out on the travel document tied to a pending I-485 triggers the three-year bar instead of being the safe exit it was for thirteen years. The escape hatch on the failure path just closed. ✅ Against all of that, here is the one thing this route unambiguously IMPROVES, and it is not on most people’s list: standing at the border. A nonimmigrant is an applicant for admission on every single entry — inspected under INA 235 with the whole of 1182(a)’s inadmissibility grounds available afresh each time. A permanent resident is not. 8 U.S.C. 1101(a)(13)(C): “An alien lawfully admitted for permanent residence in the United States shall not be regarded as seeking an admission into the United States for purposes of the immigration laws unless the alien—” followed by six named exceptions and nothing else. And a CONDITIONAL resident gets this in full — which is the step worth checking rather than assuming. 8 U.S.C. 1186b(a)(1): an investor “shall be considered, at the time of obtaining status as an alien lawfully admitted for permanent residence, to have obtained such status on a conditional basis subject to the provisions of this section.” The conditionality attaches to the BASIS, not to the classification. So 1101(a)(13)(C) applies from the day conditional residence is granted — not from the I-829 approval two years later. The six exceptions, ranked by how much control the traveller actually has: (ii) absence from the United States “for a continuous period in excess of 180 days” — a bright line, entirely controllable, and the one to actually manage. ⚠️ This is a different 180 days from the unlawful-presence 180 above: same number, unrelated rules, and merging them is easy. (v) “has committed an offense identified in section 1182(a)(2)”, “unless since such offense the alien has been granted relief under section 1182(h) or 1229b(a)” — note “committed”, not “convicted”, a lower threshold than a conviction, and the exception that does the work wherever a criminal record exists. Whether a given offence sits inside 1182(a)(2) is a legal question, not a records question, and it is the one to put to counsel rather than to a database. Then (iii) illegal activity after departing, (iv) departure while under legal process seeking removal, (i) abandonment or relinquishment of the status, and (vi) entering at a time or place other than as designated. So the direction is not ambiguous: the route moves a traveller from “inspected as an applicant for admission every time” to “not an applicant for admission at all, subject to six named exceptions”. Three of the six are things one simply does not do; one is a day count within the traveller’s control; one is abandonment; only (v) can be true of someone who does nothing at all wrong at the border. ⚠️ Two limits, because this is a shield and not immunity. It governs whether you are regarded as seeking admission — an LPR who does fall inside an exception is then inspected with the full 1182(a) grounds available, exactly as a nonimmigrant would be. And 1101(a)(13)(C) says nothing about removability under 1227 for conduct while inside the country, which is a separate track entirely.

The proposed integrity rule would make a petition missing initial evidence a denial within 30 days and give a denied petition no priority date — it is still only proposed, and on the 6 September OMB list it had not reached OMB review, so it is unlikely to land this month. A different EB-5 rule has reached it, and that one is already final. The EB-5 fee rule, RIN 1615-AC93, is on the same 6 September list as a Final Rule received 19 August 2026 — 18 days in review, against a median of 34 days for the 152 reviews open that day. It matters because 8 CFR 103.2(a)(7)(ii)(D) makes the wrong fee a rejection ground, and a rejection keeps no filing date and cannot be appealed. Ask what the cheque should say on the morning it is posted, not the week before. And the exit door is being rebuilt while he stands in front of it. Today the 180-day retention rule at 8 CFR 205.1(a)(3)(iii)(C) covers EB-5 on its plain words — DHS admits “the plain language of the regulatory text made no such distinction” — so an approved petition withdrawn later than that stays approved, and DHS says it has had “challenges in accepting and resolving requests from immigrant investors to withdraw”. That is not academic: DHS’s own reason for changing it is that “the investor’s ability to recover his or her investment may be predicated on USCIS … concluding action on his or her petition.” The proposal carves EB-5 out by name and adds a new (E) under which withdrawal revokes the approval at any time. So the same change that unlocks the money removes the protection a filed I-485 has from a withdrawal-based revocation. Both directions at once, and only one of them is the money. And there is one asymmetry here that runs the opposite way to intuition. Whether the petition is pending or approved when it fails decides what can be done about it, in the proposed rule’s own words: USCIS “will either revoke the approval of an EB-5 immigrant visa petition … or deny a pending EB-5 immigrant visa petition”. A denial of a pending petition is appealable and its merits are reviewable in court. A revocation of an approved one is not reviewable at all — Bouarfa v. Mayorkas, 604 U.S. 6 (2024). So a slower adjudication keeps him in the branch that has a remedy for longer, and an early approval moves him into the branch that has none. The standard for that revocation is also lower than it sounds: the appeal body recites that “the realization that the initial approval of the petition was in error may, in and of itself, be good and sufficient cause” for revoking it.

Two scores, never averaged

Capital risk asks whether the money comes back. Petition risk asks whether the green card arrives. They are governed by different bodies of law and only one of them has a financial regulator. A project can repay every dollar and still fail the job test; it can create every job and still lose the money. Blending them into one number hides exactly the thing worth knowing.

Higher is better on both, and the bar fills as the number rises. 65 would be a project with nothing left to find. 31 is the best capital number on this board, and it is not a good number — read the scale as “how much has survived checking”, not as a grade.

The two badges on the numbers

measured means the figure was read straight off a document or a government file. estimated means it was computed here from figures in those documents, and it is marked so you can re-run the arithmetic. Estimated does not mean doubtful — it is often more rigorous than the sponsor’s own rounded number. It means arithmetic rather than quotation.

Why so many “open” rather than “fail”

65 of the 176 gate cells on this board are open — 37% — against 17 outright failures. That is the finding, not a reassurance: almost nothing has been disproved because almost nothing has been shown. An open gate is a document nobody has produced, not an answer that came back badly. Read the open count, not the pass count.

The one that passes most gates is still not the best deal

Four projects now clear seven of these eight, and one of them — Moo Dairy — is a reject. Gates measure paperwork, not quality. They test whether the thing that must be true has been written down somewhere a reader can check, and a project can pass every one of them and still be a bad place for the money. Read them alongside the two scores, never instead of them. Where a project passes few gates it is usually because nobody has produced the documents, not because the answers came back badly — an unanswered question is not a failed one.

What is actually negotiable

Derived, not asserted. A term is negotiable here where materially similar deals in this same set carry materially different values for it — and on two of these the sponsor has the paperwork already drafted. Where a term does not vary, it is called fixed in this sample, which is fourteen deals and not a market.

The administrative fee — and two sponsors have the paperwork already drafted

This is the hardest evidence in the file. 010 Moo Dairy Farms supplied, in its own investor folder, a pre-drafted side-letter template taking the administrative fee to US$0 — a form with bracketed placeholders for date, name and email, not a one-off. Its asking price is the coupon: 2.0% down to 0.5%. Break-even is $80,000 ÷ $12,000 a year = 6.67 years, so on the four-year headline term the trade is worth about +$32,000 — but the party holding the extension switch is the sponsor, which can extend an unlimited number of times, so the counterparty controls the only variable that decides whether the trade pays. Separately, another sponsor’s own offering memorandum lets the manager modify the fee by side letter and says expressly that it need not disclose the existence or terms of any side letter to other investors. Other people are getting discounts nobody will tell you about. Ask for one, in writing, before subscribing.

The return rate — it varies twentyfold across otherwise identical structures

0.25% on seven funds — 002, 003, 006, 008, 013, 014 and 019 — 0.50% on 015, 016 and 018, 2.0% on 010 and 5.0% on 007. The 5.0% is the equity tranche of the same Brandon project whose loan tranche, 006, pays 0.25%: same sponsor, same asset, same month. A term that moves 475 basis points between two tranches of one deal is a term, not a market. Ask what rate is available, and ask for it to accrue from the date of your wire rather than from deployment.

What the return accrues on, and when the clock starts

This is worth more than the headline rate and nobody negotiates it. As drafted, the rate runs only on the money actually advanced to the borrower, only from the date it is advanced, and fund administration costs are deducted from it “but not below 0.0%”. Ask for accrual on the full $880,000 from the date of the wire. That one change is worth more than doubling the stated rate.

When the term starts — and it is almost never the day you pay

Two funds run the clock from deployment to the borrower, not from your subscription. One matures on a fixed calendar date, 31 December 2028 — subscribe in September 2026 and the initial term is about 27 months, not three years. Another runs from a loan agreement dated 25 February 2026, months before you would sign anything. Ask for the maturity to be expressed as a period from your capital contribution.

Who owns the extension — currently, not you

Extensions belong to the borrower or the sponsor on every fund here. One can be extended an unlimited number of times at the lender’s option, where the lender is the fund the sponsor controls. Ask for a hard outside date, or for the coupon to step up on each extension. A step-up is the ask most likely to be granted, because it costs the sponsor nothing unless they are late.

Whether the fee is refunded if the petition is denied — currently it is not

On every fund that discloses it, the $80,000 is non-refundable in every scenario, including a full I-526E denial with the $800,000 returned. One says so in terms: “Notwithstanding a situation where the Investing Member is entitled to a full refund of his or her Capital Contribution… the Expense Amounts shall be non-refundable.” So the floor loss on a denial is $80,000 plus government and legal fees, even where the guaranty performs perfectly. Ask for the fee to be held in escrow until the I-526E is receipted, or refunded on denial.

Do not spend a question on these

Not negotiable, and do not spend a question on them: the $800,000 itself, the ten-jobs test, the sustainment period and the reserved-category rules are all statute. The escrow release trigger is usually set by an agreement with a bank that is already executed.

So can anything still go wrong? Yes — four things, and the statute says so

It does not protect the money.

Wohali is the completed experiment. EB5AN marketed it in April 2024 quoting USCIS’s own approval of the I-956F exemplar and “over 690 qualifying EB-5 jobs”. Chapter 11 was filed sixteen months later, on 8 August 2025 (Bankr. D. Utah 25-24610), with 99 investors’ $79.2m inside it. 80 of the 99 hold I-526E approvals. The approval did its job on the visa side and was silent on the capital, which is the half that failed.

It does not approve your petition.

The project is adjudicated; you are not. Source of funds, path of funds and whether the capital is genuinely at risk are decided separately at the I-526E, and source of funds is the stated ground in 25 of the 41 post-RIA I-526E appeal decisions read for this file. An approved project with a defective funds trail is a denial.

It does not cover more investors than it approved.

The notice names a number. Bay Creek was approved for 50 investors and is marketed at 175, with no amended Form D on file. Texas Infrastructure’s notice asserts 197 in a recital and names no count in its operative sentence, against investors and $157.6m while the offering sells more than that. Selling past the approval needs an amendment, and an amendment is a new queue — read the investor count on the face of the notice, not the raise on the website.

It does not survive a material change.

Carve-out (III) is the live one. It is not a theory: on this board an approval covering a 189-key hotel at $493.6m sits against a May 2026 flyer advertising 217 keys, and another offering’s Supplement No. 1 admits investors at $200,000 against an approval built on $800,000. If a change is material, the binding effect fails for everyone in the offering, not just the investor who noticed.

So why is it still current?

Because a filing is not yet demand. The bulletin allocates numbers to “documentarily qualified applicants” — and this month it allocated against “demand received by August 10th”. What that lag means for the timing of a filing

The empty 2% lane is not an opening — a government has to be the borrower

265 visas a year, zero petitions, and it is not an oversight. The statute defines the category so narrowly that no investor can reach it by choosing it. 8 U.S.C. 1153(b)(5)(D)(iv): an “infrastructure project” means a capital investment project “in a filed or approved business plan, which is administered by a governmental entity (such as a Federal, State, or local agency or authority) that is the job-creating entity contracting with a regional center or new commercial enterprise… as financing for maintaining, improving, or constructing a public works project.” So a city, State or federal agency must itself be the borrower and the job-creating entity, inside a business plan a regional centre has already filed. Every one of those four elements sits with the sponsor and the government, not with the investor. And there is no offsetting advantage: the investment amount is the same $800,000 as rural, while rural carries statutory priority processing that this category does not. Read the empty lane as a category almost nobody can build in, not as 265 visas going spare.

The window is open because the wave has not landed

Rural is C today for the same reason it is oversubscribed on paper: the petitions exist but have not been approved. That gap is the window in which an I-485 can be filed from inside the country, and it closes when adjudication catches up, not when demand appears.

Position in the queue is set by filing date, permanently

When a cut-off date does appear, it is “the priority date of the first applicant who could not be reached within the numerical limits” — and the priority date is the day the I-526E was filed. Everyone who files after him is behind him, for good. Within the September rush, a filing on the 10th outranks the same filing on the 29th.

The two open questions, now answered from the statute

Does the 7% per-country cap apply inside the rural set-aside?

Yes, and there is no carve-out for it. The cap in 8 U.S.C. 1152(a)(2) covers every visa issued “under subsections (a) and (b) of section 1153”, and searching that whole section for an EB-5 reserved-visa exception returns nothing. But it is conditional, and that condition is the important part: under 1152(a)(5)(A), where the visas available in an employment category “exceed the number of qualified immigrants who may otherwise be issued such visas”, they are issued “without regard to” the per-country limit. So the cap is switched off while the unit is undersubscribed and switches back on the moment it is not. Rural is current for India today because supply still exceeds adjudicated demand — not because India is exempt. What the cap is worth, once it switches on — on one of two readings. No country may take more than 7% of the unit’s visas in a year. If the unit is the rural reserve, seven per cent of its 2,646 visas is 185 a year for India-born applicants; India’s own filings annualise to ~1,768 investors, so one year of Indian rural filing is about 9.6 years of India’s own capped allocation, before a single spouse or child. Both inputs are measured in the tables above; the division is the part the page had not printed, and it is the magnitude behind the phrase “the wait gets longer” — not months, high single-digit years. But which unit the switch-off runs on is not settled, and it is the single discriminator on this whole question. Start by dropping the word “category”: 8 U.S.C. 1152 never uses it. Across the whole section — 29,562 characters of statutory text — “categor…” appears zero times, against 23 occurrences of “paragraph” in the same read. The operative sentence is: “If the total number of visas available under paragraph (1), (2), (3), (4), or (5) of section 1153(b)… exceeds the number of qualified immigrants who may otherwise be issued such visas, the visas made available under that paragraph shall be issued without regard to the numerical limitation…” So the antecedent is an enumerated five-member list at paragraph level, and nothing anywhere in 1152 has subparagraph granularity. The reserves are drafted one level down, as set-asides of “the visas made available under this paragraph” inside paragraph (5). The drafting history points the same way. 1152(a)(5) was added by Pub. L. 106–313 in 2000, and the amendment notes for the whole of 1152 stop there — it has never been touched since. In 2000 paragraph (5) already had a set-aside inside it: the pre-2022 text of 1153(b)(5)(B) was headed “Set-aside for targeted employment areas” and read “Not less than 3,000 of the visas made available under this paragraph in each fiscal year shall be reserved…” So Congress picked “paragraph” as the unit while a reserve already sat one level below it, using that same formula. Run the switch-off at paragraph level and the reserve has no separate India ceiling at all; run it at reserve level and 185 is the number. The arithmetic above is the adverse branch, shown because it is the one worth planning against — it is not established. And 846 of 846 current set-aside cells over 54 monthly charts cannot settle it either — final-action designated in 30 of those 54, including all 20 June-to-September observations — because both readings predict exactly that while no reserve has ever been oversubscribed worldwide — that is a switched-off provision, not a construed one. ✔ That 846 now carries the control it needs, re-run 8 September 2026: the same parser, over the same tables in the same 54 charts, reads India’s UNRESERVED cell as something other than ‘C’ in 48 of 54 months, taking 13 distinct values — dates from 01APR17 through 01MAY24, and U in the last three. So the instrument demonstrably can read a cut-off, forty-eight times, and finds none in the reserves. Without that control, 846 of 846 is indistinguishable from a reader that only knows how to output C. The chart history begins at the first month the reserves appear at all — April 2022 — and all three reserved rows are present in every one of the 54, so the run has no gap to hide a cut-off in. ⚠ And the denominator must be read for what it is: 846 cells are not 846 independent trials. Consecutive months are strongly autocorrelated — a category current in August is overwhelmingly likely to be current in September — so the effective sample is far smaller than 846, and a clean run says the event has not been a normal monthly occurrence rather than that it cannot occur. The unreserved series is the warning attached to that: India has carried a cut-off there in most months since October 2022, and it moved 01MAY24 → 01MAY22 → U across April to July 2026. The unreserved door has been shut or heavily retrogressed for an India-chargeable filer throughout; the reserves are the only open route, and the fact that they have never closed is a base rate, not a guarantee. The best authority that exists is favourable, and it is dicta. Babaria v. Blinken, 87 F.4th 963 (9th Cir., 1 December 2023, No. 22-16700, published, and this is the circuit a California filer sits in) describes the provision this way: “two key provisions apply when an employment preference category is undersubscribed. One lifts the seven-percent country cap in any calendar quarter where ‘the total number of visas available’ in a particular preference category ‘exceeds the number of qualified immigrants who may otherwise be issued such visas.’” A preference category is one of the five paragraphs, which is the benign reading. Two limits, both real: it was an EB-2 and EB-3 case, so no set-aside was before the court — “EB-5” appears zero times in all 40 pages — and the sentence is background, not holding. What the same passage does establish as fact is how much the switch-off is worth: the exception “has been applied every year since 1990”, and in FY2021 Indian nationals used 47% of all EB-2 visas and 27% of all EB-3 — far above seven per cent, because while a unit is undersubscribed the cap simply does not operate. One published cell would resolve it: the India column of the rural set-aside row, in the first month the same row’s all-countries cell is not current. This is a written question for immigration counsel, not something the statute answers on its face — and counsel who calls it settled either way should be asked which primary source says so.

What happens to rural visas nobody uses?

They stay rural for one more year. 8 U.S.C. 1153(b)(5)(B)(i)(II): unused reserved visas “shall remain available within the same category for the immediately succeeding fiscal year”, and only if still unissued at the end of that year do they fall into the general pool. So 1 October does not merely reset the rural allocation, it tops it up with whatever FY2026 did not use.

How many visas does one investor actually consume?

Still not established, and it is the one number that would sharpen everything above. A visa is used by the investor and by each accompanying spouse and child. Three routes to the real ratio were tried and all three failed in the same session: the State Department annual report is refused to this network, the Department of Homeland Security yearbook tables return not-found at every published path, and two search engines answered with bot checks rather than results. So it is left unmeasured rather than guessed.

Why the missing number does not change the answer

The conclusion does not depend on that missing number. The multiplier cannot be less than one — the investor always consumes a visa. At exactly one, an impossible best case in which not a single investor brings a spouse or a child, the annualised rural filings are ~4,244 investors against 2,646 visas, already 1.6× oversubscribed. Every real value makes it worse and none makes it better, so the direction is settled and only the severity is unknown.

So will rural be current in the October bulletin?

Expect yes — and the reason is a timing mismatch, not optimism. The bulletin counts only documentarily qualified applicants, meaning approved petitions; the 2,122 rural filings from the first half of FY2026 are overwhelmingly still unadjudicated and so are not yet demand, while 1 October adds a fresh 20% allocation plus every unused FY2026 rural visa carried forward in the same category — so supply rises on the one date that demand cannot. The retrogression is real but it arrives when adjudication catches up with the filing wave rather than when the wave is filed, which points at FY2027 or FY2028; the single thing that could pull it forward is the priority processing rural petitions receive by statute, which is precisely a mechanism for converting filings into adjudicated demand faster than anywhere else. ⚠️ And there is now direct evidence, from a sponsor on this board, that the statutory rural priority is not reliably delivered. The regional centre behind PRJ-013 sued USCIS for a writ of mandamus (D. Idaho 1:25-cv-00614, 24 Oct 2025) pleading that its own rural I-956F had sat over five months while others were decided in 78 days or less, and that “the RIA mandates priority processing for petitions associated with rural projects such as Plaintiff’s”. The detail is on that project’s card. For this section the consequence is one line: rural priority is a statutory entitlement that has had to be enforced in federal court, not a service level to plan around. It weakens the mechanism that would pull retrogression forward — and equally, it weakens any reliance on rural filings being converted to demand quickly.

Form I-956F — which projects USCIS has actually approved

Every sponsor on this board says “approved”. The word covers four different positions, and the difference decides whether an I-526E filed this month can ever be approved — a petition cannot be approved until the project’s I-956F is. There is no public register of I-956F approvals, so holding the notice is the ceiling of what anyone outside USCIS can establish, and roughly a fifth of applications are refused. Receipt numbers and dates are in the right-hand column, not hidden in a tooltip.

I-797 in hand7The approval notice itself was read: receipt number, date, and the exact fund being sold named on it as the new commercial enterprise.
Notice, with a gap3A notice is in hand and it does not cleanly cover what is on sale — approved for a smaller offering, or two copies of one receipt number that contradict each other.
Stated in the offering1The approval is asserted in the offering document itself, with a date — a securities disclosure rather than a brochure line — but the notice is not reproduced, so the receipt number and the fund named on it are unseen.
Claimed only2The sponsor asserts approval in marketing. No notice, no receipt number, no filing date in any document supplied.
No approval9No approval is claimed at all. Where a sponsor claims one for a sibling fund in the same email and not for this one, the silence is the finding.
What an I-797 is, and how to check one

Form I-797 is the paper USCIS sends when it approves something — the Notice of Action. “I-797 in hand” below means the project’s actual approval notice has been read, receipt number and date included, not that a sponsor said one exists. 7 of the 22 are at that standard. To check one yourself: ask for the notice as a PDF and confirm three things on its face — the fund named on it is the exact entity taking the money, the date, and the number of investors it was approved for, since at least one project was approved for 50 and then marketed at 170 with the amendment still pending. A receipt number typed into an email is not a notice. Not established: whether USCIS’s public case-status tool accepts an INF-prefixed I-956F receipt — the tool sits behind a bot check that could not be cleared from here, so that is a two-minute manual test rather than a verified route.

The money leaves before USCIS looks at the petition

On all five EB5AN funds escrow releases on filing, not on approval. The $800,000 and the $80,000 administration fee are gone from your control the day the petition is receipted, and the administration fee is forfeited in full on a source-of-funds denial — which is the denial that depends on your own file rather than on any sponsor. Board-wide count, and it is unanimous: 0 of the 22 offerings condition release on conditional permanent residence. The release trigger has actually been read and quoted in 4 of the 22 — 002, 003, 008 and 015 — and all four release on filing. ⚠️ The other 18 are not stated in the file, which is not the same as not existing: the trigger may sit in a PPM nobody has produced, so read 17 as unexamined rather than as filing-based. ⚠️ One of the four is a trap worth naming, because a keyword read of it gives the wrong answer. 008’s Escrow Agreement §4 conditions release on the Form I-956 being approved, the I-956F having “been filed”, and “the I-526E Petition for a Subscriber has been filed with USCIS”. The word approved attaches to the I-956 — the regional centre’s own designation — not to your petition. Scanning for “approv” near “escrow” reads that as approval-based release. It is not. It is filing. 003 is the one that shows the mechanism: its Escrow Agreement carries two competing release paths — an approval-based one (“a Subscriber’s I-526E Petition has been approved by USCIS, the Escrow Agent shall disburse…”) and a filing-based one introduced by “Notwithstanding anything to the contrary”, under a PPM heading that says it outright: “Release of Subscription Amount from Escrow Prior to I-526E Petition Approval”. The filing path controls. And releasing early destroys a remedy: “in the event that the denied Subscriber’s Subscription Proceeds were released from the Escrow Account, no such substitution shall be made” (Escrow §4(e)), with refunds “without deduction or payment of interest”. Now the part that reframes all of it: the law would allow the opposite, and nobody sells it. Proposed 8 CFR 204.401 would codify the outer limit — “Actively in the process of investing means an actual commitment to invest… no later than the date on which the investor obtains conditional permanent resident status. Such capital may be held in escrow for release to the new commercial enterprise no later than the date on which the investor obtains conditional permanent resident status.” So escrow may lawfully run all the way to the green card. Every offering here uses the earliest permissible moment; the rule permits the latest. Where a set-aside category is retrogressed, the gap between those two dates is the whole wait for a visa number — years in which capital could lawfully have stayed recoverable and does not. ⚠️ And the same document says two different things. The preamble repeatedly describes escrow as running “pending approval of his or her EB-5 immigrant visa petition” and speaks of “after the approval… and the investor’s capital is released from escrow”, while the regulatory text sets the bound at conditional permanent residence — which in a retrogressed category arrives years later. Two different dates in one rulemaking, and the codified text is the one that would bind. A sponsor asked for escrow-to-CPR can point at the preamble to refuse; the answer is the proposed definition.

The ten jobs are a number from a model — and since 2022 there is a hard deadline on them

Asked plainly: if the jobs have not been created, is it enough that someone runs the formula? For a regional-centre investor, substantially yes. The ten jobs can be output from an economic model, and nobody ever has to name ten employees. USCIS’s own Form I-829 says so on its face: Part 8, Item 2.a asks for the “Number of Full-Time Economically Direct, Indirect and Induced Jobs Created” — “economically direct” is a modelling term, not a payroll term — and Item 5 asks, if ten jobs “have not yet been created”, for the number “expected to be created within a reasonable time”. The government printed a box for jobs that do not exist yet. That is the single biggest reason regional-centre EB-5 is easier than direct EB-5, and it is genuine. But three things break it, and they are where money is actually lost. First, the money has to have been spent. The model is accepted; its inputs are audited. Construction expenditure that never happened produces no modelled jobs, so a stalled project does not simply create jobs more slowly — it stops creating them. Second, and this is the one no marketing deck mentions: there is now a hard outer deadline. The jobs must exist by the third anniversary of the conditional green card, or conditional status terminates by operation of statute — INA 216A(c)(3)(B)(ii)(II)(bb), added by the 2022 reform. The old open-ended “reasonable time” is gone for anyone filing today. Third, since 15 March 2024 a physical site visit is a statutory precondition to removing conditions, under INA 216A(c)(1)(C). Someone from the government goes and looks. Put those together and the risk on this board is not that a model is wrong. It is that spending stops while the clock keeps running — which is precisely the shape of the one documented failure involving EB5AN. And there is a fourth thing, which is the one an actual adjudication turned on: the jobs are a pool, and investors are paid out of it in order. Under 8 CFR 204.6(g)(2) jobs already allocated to earlier investors are subtracted before yours are counted. ⚠️ And read the indirect-jobs cap correctly, because the natural reading is the wrong one. The statute caps the share of the whole ten-job requirement that indirect jobs may fill — not the share of the construction-derived jobs. 8 U.S.C. 1153(b)(5)(E)(iv)(I): indirect jobs may satisfy “only up to 90 percent of the requirement under subparagraph (A)(ii)”. And (E)(iv)(II): “If the jobs estimated to be created are created by construction activity lasting less than 2 years”, that ceiling drops to “only up to 75 percent of the requirement under subparagraph (A)(ii)”. So a short build does not merely discount its own construction jobs — it tightens the cap on the entire requirement, and it cannot be worked around by pointing at jobs from somewhere else. In practice: at 90% at least one job in ten must be direct; at 75% at least two and a half in ten must be, and proposed 8 CFR 204.421(b) would separately require “at least one job per regional center investor” to be a direct job. A 2011 decision on a regional-centre dairy shows the arithmetic in the government’s own words: 14 direct jobs at the approved 2.66 multiplier gave 37.24, minus 13 filled by non-qualifying employees gave 24.24, minus “the 20 jobs that have been allocated” to two investors who had already removed conditions, so “we could not allocate more than four of the indirect jobs to the petitioner”. Order: petition denied. A project 20% short left the last investor 58% short. ⚠️ And the short-build penalty is TWO separate rules, not one. Reading it as one understates the arithmetic. (E)(iv)(II) tightens the cap on the whole requirement from 90% to 75%, as above. A different clause — 8 U.S.C. 1153(b)(5)(E)(v)(II)(cc), headed “Construction activity jobs” — then shrinks the numerator: “If the number of direct jobs estimated to be created has been determined by an economically and statistically valid methodology, and such direct jobs are created by construction activity lasting less than 2 years, the number of such jobs that may be considered direct jobs for purposes of clause (iv) shall be calculated by multiplying the total number of such jobs estimated to be created by the fraction of the 2-year period that the construction activity lasts.” So an 18-month build multiplies its direct construction jobs by 0.75 AND caps indirect at 75% — the two penalties compound, and they are found in different subparagraphs. 🔴 Which raises the question nothing else here answers: if the schedule later changes, does the approved project application still protect the investor? On the plain text, no — not automatically. 8 U.S.C. 1153(b)(5)(F)(ii) is the binding-effect clause, and it is generous in one respect worth knowing: approval “shall be binding for purposes of the adjudication of subsequent petitions… by immigrants investing in the same offering… and of petitions by the same immigrants filed under section 1186b” — 1186b is the removal-of-conditions provision, so the protection is written to reach the I-829, years later. But it is expressly conditional: “unless… (III) there has been a material change that affects eligibility” (the other four exits are fraud, public-safety or national-security concerns, undisclosed evidence, and a material mistake of law or fact). A duration change affects eligibility by both of the routes just described — it moves the direct-job multiplier and it moves the cap — so a build that slips across 24 months is not a scheduling detail, it is the exception that switches the binding effect off. ⚠️ The cure exists, and it belongs to the sponsor, not to the investor. (F)(iii)(I) lets a centre seek an amendment “not later than 30 days after any such changes”; (F)(iii)(II) then says that on approval of a timely filed amendment the changes “may be incorporated into and considered in determining program eligibility” for pending petitions. May, not shall. Proposed 8 CFR 204.424(c)(5) would codify the same 30-day duty for changes “materially impacting the credibility or viability of such plans that could adversely impact eligibility for associated investors”. And a separate clock applies to the centre itself: (E)(vi)(I)(aa) requires notice “not later than 120 days before the implementation of significant proposed changes to its organizational structure, ownership, or administration”. So the investor carries the eligibility risk while only the sponsor can file the fix, inside 30 days, and even then incorporation is discretionary. How exposed is this board? A duration is stated in 11 of the 22 offerings and 10 state none; 022 has not been classified on this axis. Of those that do, the files put 001 at ~9–12 months (its own scorecard flags the double penalty 🔴), 016 at 29 months, 014 at ~28, 003 at ~60 and 008 at 81. Two sit on the line itself: 004 is contested inside its own file — 27 months on the deck’s March 2026 start, 20 months if the genuine start is October 2026, and the file records that both readings cannot hold; and 006 is at exactly 24 months (June 2026 to June 2028), scored as clearing S2 with the note “Exactly 24 months, USCIS accepted ‘2 years’”. Exactly 24 is zero headroom: a one-day slip crosses into the double penalty, and whether the approved application still shields the petition then depends on a sponsor filing an amendment within 30 days. ⚠️ Read the 10 with no stated duration as unexamined, not as safe. 🔴 And the ordering rule is the single most important sentence on this page for a filer going in on the last day. The current regulation supplies no ordering rule at all — it defers to “any reasonable agreement” among the investors, so an agreement written pro rata spreads a shortfall instead of concentrating it. The proposed rule supplies a default, and the default is the worst possible one for him: “If no such agreement exists, USCIS will allocate job creation based on the filing date of the EB-5 immigrant visa petition” (proposed 8 CFR 204.407(e)(2)). Filing on 17 September 2026 puts him behind every earlier investor in the same offering, unless the offering documents contain an allocation agreement that says otherwise. And the proposed rule spells out what being behind actually looks like, which is not a denial. Where established job creation is “less than the amount needed to satisfy the job creation requirement for all associated regional center investors”, USCIS allocates by that filing-date rule and issues a notice of partial approval — proposed 8 CFR 204.423(b)(3)(ii). The investors inside the allocation are approved; the ones outside it are left to a further amendment by the regional centre and to the one-year extension at proposed 216.6(g)(3), against the three-year statutory job deadline. So a shortfall does not fail the project. It sorts the investors, and the sort key is the date on the receipt. So the question is not “how many jobs per investor”. It is four numbers, and the third one decides the other three: total modelled jobs; the maximum investor count the offering permits; what the allocation clause actually says; and how many investors have already been allocated theirs. On this board only the first is ever published. One thing runs the other way, and it is worth knowing. The methodology approved at I-526E is locked in for the I-829 — the appeal body, applying Chang v. United States, 327 F.3d 911 (9th Cir. 2003), held the director “erred in revisiting the appropriateness of the multiplier” and that the investor “should be able to rely on” it. So the modelling risk is fixed at the front end, which is exactly why the pool-and-position questions have to be asked before signing rather than after. Two more requirements that are easy to miss because they are stated in different places. Proposed 8 CFR 204.407(e)(3) sets the floor at “at least ten (10) qualifying employees per regional center investor” — ten times the investor count and nothing more. And proposed 204.421(b) adds a separate one: “At least one job per regional center investor must be a direct job”, so a project whose whole count is indirect and induced does not qualify however large the model output. The words buffer, cushion, excess job and surplus appear zero times in the 127-page proposed rule — but DHS does name the idea in its own vocabulary, contemplating “additional jobs that are unnecessary for investor eligibility” at proposed 204.423(c)(5). So the 20–30% cushion is a market convention with no numeric anchor in the rules, not a concept the rules have never heard of.

The one protection every offering advertises, and no public record can verify for any of them

Every sponsor in this market markets a money-back guarantee on I-526E approval, and none of them names the guarantor. A competitor’s marketing, quoted in a court exhibit, is the clean example: “Investor Protections: Behring offers a money-back guaranty on I-526E approval, further minimizing risk for EB-5 participants.” Read that sentence for who is legally bound and it says nothing — “Behring offers” is a brand, not an entity with a balance sheet. The distinction is not pedantry. It is the whole value of the guarantee. In the single EB-5 deal anywhere whose executed loan agreement can be read in public, the job-creation guaranty and the I-526E refund guaranty both run from the borrower — the contract literally styles the developer as “Job Creation Guarantor” and “I-526E Approval Refund Guarantor”. So the protection is written by the party whose failure is the event it insures against, and it is worth nothing at the only moment it is needed. Now the part that makes this universal rather than one sponsor’s problem. Searching the whole federal court record for those exact contract terms returns five documents and two documents, and every one is that same bankruptcy — against a positive control that returned a full page for “I-526E” and a negative control that returned nothing. That is not evidence the rest of the industry does it differently. Court records hold documents somebody paid to buy, and offering documents are almost never filed at all. That one deal is readable only because it collapsed and its contract became an exhibit. So for every offering on this board the guarantor is unverifiable from any public source, and the only way to learn it before signing is to ask — name the guaranteeing entity, and give its audited net worth with a date. It is one sentence to answer and it cannot be answered any other way.

What happens when an EB-5 project fails

Every offering document describes a plan. This is the only one in this research where the ending is already on the public record — a fund of 99 investors at $800,000 each, secured by a recorded first mortgage on 1,377 acres, whose borrower filed for bankruptcy. It is worth more than any brochure, because it shows what the protections actually do.

Everything below is quoted from two court documents: the debtor’s sworn schedules, filed 22 August 2025, and the settlement agreement the court approved on 24 March 2026. No sponsor supplied either one and neither can be edited by anyone with an interest in how it reads.

This was the structure you are told to want

Not a mezzanine slice, not preferred equity, not an unsecured note. A loan agreement dated 11 August 2022 for up to $79,200,000, funded by up to 99 investors at $800,000 each. Security was a Deed of Trust, Assignment of Rents and Leases, Security Agreement and Fixture Filing, executed 31 October 2022 and recorded on 9 November 2022 in two counties — Summit County entry 01197541, book 2763, page 1333, and Morgan County entry 162312, book 396, page 599. The collateral ran to twenty-three Summit County parcels and six in Morgan County, taking in the land, the buildings, the rents and the water rights. The debtor’s own filing lists the EB-5 fund first in priority. If you had read this offering in 2022 and checked the recorder, you would have found exactly what you were promised.

What happened anyway

  1. Nov 2022 – Nov 2024

    Money goes out the door across two years.

  2. 13 May 2025

    After sequential notices of default, the fund accelerates: the loan is terminated, no further money will be advanced, everything is due now.

  3. 8 August 2025

    The borrower files Chapter 11 in the District of Utah.

  4. 11 September 2025

    The fund moves on an emergency basis to have a trustee appointed, i.e. to take the company out of its own management’s hands. The court grants it on 25 September.

  5. 14 November 2025

    The trustee asks the court to let the fund lend the estate new money — up to $6.3m, later raised to $6.8m — including to pay a public infrastructure district assessment.

  6. 27 January 2026

    The trustee gives notice that he intends to challenge the fund’s own claim.

  7. 17–24 March 2026

    Private mediation. It settles on the last day.

What the fund actually got

Not money. Land. The settlement has the fund bidding for the borrower’s assets with the debt itself — a credit bid of $65,000,000, made up of the new loan first (about $7,000,000) and then $58,000,000 of the original claim, with the right to go up to $89,000,000 at auction. It adds $1,800,000 in cash to pay the administrative costs of the bankruptcy. The shortfall — the difference between the $86.2m owed and what it bids — is capped by agreement at $10,000,000 and ranks behind nothing but ahead of nothing, as an ordinary unsecured claim.

Where it leaves the investors

So the end state is this: ninety-nine people’s $78.87m has become a part-built golf and residential development in Utah that the fund now owns and has to finish, market and sell. There is no repayment date, because there is no repayment. There is a disposal.

What this teaches that no offering document will

1
A first lien buys control, not repayment.

The fund did everything a secured lender is supposed to do, in the right order and quickly: notices of default, acceleration, an emergency application for a trustee, rescue financing, a credit bid. The machinery worked exactly as designed. It still ends with the fund owning the land instead of holding the cash. When a sponsor says “we hold a first mortgage”, that is true and it is worth having — it is just not the same sentence as “you get your money back”.

2
Defending the lien cost roughly $13m of new money.

Up to $6.8m of rescue lending, $1.8m of cash at closing, a $4m guarantee to the other creditors backed by a standby letter of credit, and up to $400,000 to stop the golf course deteriorating. None of that was in the $79.2m the investors subscribed. A fund that cannot write this cheque loses the collateral to somebody who can. Ask every sponsor: if the borrower defaults, who funds the enforcement, out of what, and does drawing it dilute me? It is not a question any offering document answers on its own.

3
The trustee attacked the EB-5 fund’s claim itself.

Before mediation the trustee set out claims against the fund for constructive fraudulent transfer, equitable subordination, recharacterisation and unjust enrichment — and the settlement records, in terms, that these “could impact” the claim. Recharacterisation means a court treating what you called a loan as equity. Equitable subordination means pushing a secured lender behind the trade creditors it was ahead of. Both were live enough to settle. The fund bought a release, and the price shows in the gap between the $86.2m claim and the $58m it bid. This is the standing risk to every loan-model EB-5 deal and it is almost never disclosed as one.

4
A district assessment got paid before the mortgage.

The very first rescue-financing motion asked for authority to pay the local public infrastructure district’s 2025 assessment, and the settlement obliges the fund to take on all of that district’s obligations along with the taxes. An assessment is not a mortgage and does not queue behind one — it is collected like a property tax. If a project you are looking at carries a C-PACE loan, a special assessment, an improvement district or a special tax, that money sits ahead of yours in practice whatever the mortgage says. One project in this file carries a $100m C-PACE disclosure; this is the evidence for why that matters, and it is evidence rather than theory.

5
Four years in, and the clock is still running.

First money out in November 2022. Acceleration May 2025. Settlement March 2026. A sale in mid-2026 at the earliest, and then a development to build and sell after that. For the immigration side this is survivable and even helpful — capital that is demonstrably still at risk is exactly what the second petition needs. For a person who planned around getting the money back in year five, it is not.

What to put to a sponsor because of this

  1. If the borrower defaults, what is the source of enforcement capital, is it committed in writing, and does drawing on it dilute or subordinate me?
  2. Has any trustee, receiver or creditors’ committee ever asserted recharacterisation or equitable subordination against a fund you manage or have managed? Name every one.
  3. Does the collateral sit behind any assessment district, C-PACE lien, special tax or improvement district? Name each and state the balance outstanding.
  4. What is the current loan-to-value on the collateral, on whose appraisal, and dated when?
  5. Does the fund’s governing document let the manager commit further investor capital to protect the lien without a vote?

This is one fund and one borrower, and a bad outcome for one project is not a verdict on a sponsor or on the programme. It is used here for the opposite reason: it is the only place in this research where the machinery can be watched working, so it is the only place the abstract protections can be priced. Read it as a specimen, not as an indictment.

Method

Every document reachable from any sponsor was collected and made machine-readable — 148 source documents in all, including roughly 3,000 pages of scans put through optical character recognition so they could be searched rather than skimmed. Marketing decks, offering memoranda, loan agreements, partnership agreements, guaranties, escrow agreements, economic reports and government approval notices were read against each other and against records held by parties with no interest in the outcome: the immigration agency’s own approval notices and its two published regional-centre lists, securities filings read directly from the regulator, federal court records, county land and property records, and local reporting.

Three rules were applied throughout. A confidence label belongs to the quotation, never to the conclusion drawn from it — a verbatim quote with a wrong inference on top is the failure that survives review, because the evidence underneath it is real. Every stated absence carries a control that could have failed; an uncontrolled “not found” is a guess wearing a credential it did not earn. And where a sponsor’s own documents disagree with each other, that disagreement is itself the finding, recorded with both figures and where each appears.

Every finding was then put through an adversarial pass whose default posture was refutation, in both directions — because wrongly clearing a bad project and wrongly condemning a sound one cost the same, and with a filing deadline close there is no time to recover from either.
