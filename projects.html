Two scores, never averaged

Capital risk asks whether the money comes back. Petition risk asks whether the green card arrives. They are governed by different bodies of law and only one of them has a financial regulator. A project can repay every dollar and still fail the job test; it can create every job and still lose the money. Blending them into one number hides exactly the thing worth knowing.

Higher is better on both, and the bar fills as the number rises. 65 would be a project with nothing left to find. 31 is the best capital number on this board, and it is not a good number — read the scale as “how much has survived checking”, not as a grade.

The two badges on the numbers

measured means the figure was read straight off a document or a government file. estimated means it was computed here from figures in those documents, and it is marked so you can re-run the arithmetic. Estimated does not mean doubtful — it is often more rigorous than the sponsor’s own rounded number. It means arithmetic rather than quotation.

Why so many “open” rather than “fail”

62 of the 176 gate cells on this board are open — 35% — against 17 outright failures. That is the finding, not a reassurance: almost nothing has been disproved because almost nothing has been shown. An open gate is a document nobody has produced, not an answer that came back badly. Read the open count, not the pass count.

The one that passes most gates is still not the best deal

Four projects now clear seven of these eight, and one of them — Moo Dairy — is a reject. Gates measure paperwork, not quality. They test whether the thing that must be true has been written down somewhere a reader can check, and a project can pass every one of them and still be a bad place for the money. Read them alongside the two scores, never instead of them. Where a project passes few gates it is usually because nobody has produced the documents, not because the answers came back badly — an unanswered question is not a failed one.

  • Separately, another sponsor’s own offering memorandum lets the manager modify the fee by side letter and says expressly that it need not disclose the existence or terms of any side letter to other investors. Other people are getting discounts nobody will tell you about.
  • Ask for one, in writing, before subscribing.

The return rate — it varies twentyfold across otherwise identical structures

  • 0.25% on seven funds — 002, 003, 006, 008, 013, 014 and 019 — 0.50% on 015, 016 and 018, 2.0% on 010 and 5.0% on 007. The 5.0% is the equity tranche of the same Brandon project whose loan tranche, 006, pays 0.25%: same sponsor, same asset, same month.
  • A term that moves 475 basis points between two tranches of one deal is a term, not a market. Ask what rate is available, and ask for it to accrue from the date of your wire rather than from deployment.

What the return accrues on, and when the clock starts

  • This is worth more than the headline rate and nobody negotiates it. As drafted, the rate runs only on the money actually advanced to the borrower, only from the date it is advanced, and fund administration costs are deducted from it “but not below 0.0%”.
  • Ask for accrual on the full $880,000 from the date of the wire. That one change is worth more than doubling the stated rate.

When the term starts — and it is almost never the day you pay

  • Two funds run the clock from deployment to the borrower, not from your subscription. One matures on a fixed calendar date, 31 December 2028 — subscribe in September 2026 and the initial term is about 27 months, not three years.
  • Another runs from a loan agreement dated 25 February 2026, months before you would sign anything. Ask for the maturity to be expressed as a period from your capital contribution.

Who owns the extension — currently, not you

  • Extensions belong to the borrower or the sponsor on every fund here. One can be extended an unlimited number of times at the lender’s option, where the lender is the fund the sponsor controls.
  • Ask for a hard outside date, or for the coupon to step up on each extension. A step-up is the ask most likely to be granted, because it costs the sponsor nothing unless they are late.

Whether the fee is refunded if the petition is denied — currently it is not

  • On every fund that discloses it, the $80,000 is non-refundable in every scenario, including a full I-526E denial with the $800,000 returned.
  • One says so in terms: “Notwithstanding a situation where the Investing Member is entitled to a full refund of his or her Capital Contribution… the Expense Amounts shall be non-refundable.” So the floor loss on a denial is $80,000 plus government and legal fees, even where the guaranty performs perfectly. Ask for the fee to be held in escrow until the I-526E is receipted, or refunded on denial.
Do not spend a question on these

Not negotiable, and do not spend a question on them: the $800,000 itself, the ten-jobs test, the sustainment period and the reserved-category rules are all statute. The escrow release trigger is usually set by an agreement with a bank that is already executed.

It does not protect the money.

It does not approve your petition.

  • The project is adjudicated; you are not. Source of funds, path of funds and whether the capital is genuinely at risk are decided separately at the I-526E, and source of funds is the stated ground in 25 of the 41 post-RIA I-526E appeal decisions read for this file.

It does not cover more investors than it approved.

  • The notice names a number. Bay Creek was approved for 50 investors and is marketed at 175, with no amended Form D on file.

What that lag means for the timing of a filing

The empty 2% lane is not an opening — a government has to be the borrower

  • 265 visas a year, zero petitions, and it is not an oversight. The statute defines the category so narrowly that no investor can reach it by choosing it. 8 U.S.C. 1153(b)(5)(D)(iv): an “infrastructure project” means a capital investment project “in a filed or approved business plan, which is administered by a governmental entity (such as a Federal, State, or local agency or authority) that is the job-creating entity contracting with a regional center or new commercial enterprise… as financing for maintaining, improving, or constructing a public works project.” So a city, State or federal agency must itself be the borrower and the job-creating entity, inside a business plan a regional centre has already filed. Every one of those four elements sits with the sponsor and the government, not with the investor.
  • And there is no offsetting advantage: the investment amount is the same $800,000 as rural, while rural carries statutory priority processing that this category does not. Read the empty lane as a category almost nobody can build in, not as 265 visas going spare.

The window is open because the wave has not landed

Rural is C today for the same reason it is oversubscribed on paper: the petitions exist but have not been approved. That gap is the window in which an I-485 can be filed from inside the country, and it closes when adjudication catches up, not when demand appears.

Position in the queue is set by filing date, permanently

  • When a cut-off date does appear, it is “the priority date of the first applicant who could not be reached within the numerical limits” — and the priority date is the day the I-526E was filed.
  • Everyone who files after him is behind him, for good. Within the September rush, a filing on the 10th outranks the same filing on the 29th.

The surge measured here is the part before the surge

  • These are receipts to March 2026. The grandfathering deadline is 30 September 2026, and the whole market is being sold on it — one sponsor’s site carries a countdown clock on every page.
  • The half-year already claims 80% of a year’s rural visas; the six months since are not in the data yet.

The two open questions, now answered from the statute

Does the 7% per-country cap apply inside the rural set-aside?

  • Yes, and there is no carve-out for it. The cap in 8 U.S.C. 1152(a)(2) covers every visa issued “under subsections (a) and (b) of section 1153”, and searching that whole section for an EB-5 reserved-visa exception returns nothing.
  • But it is conditional, and that condition is the important part: under 1152(a)(5)(A), where the visas available in an employment category “exceed the number of qualified immigrants who may otherwise be issued such visas”, they are issued “without regard to” the per-country limit.
  • So the cap is switched off while the unit is undersubscribed and switches back on the moment it is not. Rural is current for India today because supply still exceeds adjudicated demand — not because India is exempt.
  • What the cap is worth, once it switches on — on one of two readings. No country may take more than 7% of the unit’s visas in a year.
  • If the unit is the rural reserve, seven per cent of its 2,646 visas is 185 a year for India-born applicants; India’s own filings annualise to ~1,768 investors, so one year of Indian rural filing is about 9.6 years of India’s own capped allocation, before a single spouse or child. Both inputs are measured in the tables above; the division is the part the page had not printed, and it is the magnitude behind the phrase “the wait gets longer” — not months, high single-digit years.
  • But which unit the switch-off runs on is not settled, and it is the single discriminator on this whole question. Start by dropping the word “category”: 8 U.S.C. 1152 never uses it. Across the whole section — 29,562 characters of statutory text — “categor…” appears zero times, against 23 occurrences of “paragraph” in the same read.
  • The operative sentence is: “If the total number of visas available under paragraph (1), (2), (3), (4), or (5) of section 1153(b)… exceeds the number of qualified immigrants who may otherwise be issued such visas, the visas made available under that paragraph shall be issued without regard to the numerical limitation…” So the antecedent is an enumerated five-member list at paragraph level, and nothing anywhere in 1152 has subparagraph granularity. The reserves are drafted one level down, as set-asides of “the visas made available under this paragraph” inside paragraph (5).
  • The drafting history points the same way. 1152(a)(5) was added by Pub. L. 106–313 in 2000, and the amendment notes for the whole of 1152 stop there — it has never been touched since.
  • In 2000 paragraph (5) already had a set-aside inside it: the pre-2022 text of 1153(b)(5)(B) was headed “Set-aside for targeted employment areas” and read “Not less than 3,000 of the visas made available under this paragraph in each fiscal year shall be reserved…” So Congress picked “paragraph” as the unit while a reserve already sat one level below it, using that same formula. Run the switch-off at paragraph level and the reserve has no separate India ceiling at all; run it at reserve level and 185 is the number.
  • The arithmetic above is the adverse branch — worth planning against, not established. 846 of 846 current set-aside cells across 54 monthly charts cannot settle it either, because both readings predict exactly that and no reserve has ever been oversubscribed worldwide.
  • ✔ The instrument is controlled. The same parser over the same 54 charts reads India’s UNRESERVED cell as something other than ‘C’ in 48 of 54 months, taking 13 distinct values — so it can demonstrably read a cut-off, and finds none in the reserves.
  • ⚠ But 846 cells are not 846 independent trials. Consecutive months are strongly autocorrelated, so a clean run says the event has not been a normal monthly occurrence — not that it cannot occur.
  • The unreserved series is the warning attached to that: India has carried a cut-off there in most months since October 2022, and it moved 01MAY24 → 01MAY22 → U across April to July 2026.
  • The unreserved door has been shut or heavily retrogressed for an India-chargeable filer throughout; the reserves are the only open route, and the fact that they have never closed is a base rate, not a guarantee. The best authority that exists is favourable, and it is dicta. Babaria v. Blinken, 87 F.4th 963 (9th Cir., 1 December 2023, No. 22-16700, published, and this is the circuit a California filer sits in) describes the provision this way: “two key provisions apply when an employment preference category is undersubscribed. One lifts the seven-percent country cap in any calendar quarter where ‘the total number of visas available’ in a particular preference category ‘exceeds the number of qualified immigrants who may otherwise be issued such visas.’” A preference category is one of the five paragraphs, which is the benign reading.
  • Two limits, both real: it was an EB-2 and EB-3 case, so no set-aside was before the court — “EB-5” appears zero times in all 40 pages — and the sentence is background, not holding. What the same passage does establish as fact is how much the switch-off is worth: the exception “has been applied every year since 1990”, and in FY2021 Indian nationals used 47% of all EB-2 visas and 27% of all EB-3 — far above seven per cent, because while a unit is undersubscribed the cap simply does not operate.
  • One published cell would resolve it: the India column of the rural set-aside row, in the first month the same row’s all-countries cell is not current. This is a written question for immigration counsel, not something the statute answers on its face — and counsel who calls it settled either way should be asked which primary source says so.

What happens to rural visas nobody uses?

They stay rural for one more year. 8 U.S.C. 1153(b)(5)(B)(i)(II): unused reserved visas “shall remain available within the same category for the immediately succeeding fiscal year”, and only if still unissued at the end of that year do they fall into the general pool. So 1 October does not merely reset the rural allocation, it tops it up with whatever FY2026 did not use.

How many visas does one investor actually consume?

  • Still not established, and it is the one number that would sharpen everything above. A visa is used by the investor and by each accompanying spouse and child.
  • Three routes to the real ratio were tried and all three failed in the same session: the State Department annual report is refused to this network, the Department of Homeland Security yearbook tables return not-found at every published path, and two search engines answered with bot checks rather than results. So it is left unmeasured rather than guessed.
Why the missing number does not change the answer
  • The conclusion does not depend on that missing number. The multiplier cannot be less than one — the investor always consumes a visa.
  • At exactly one, an impossible best case in which not a single investor brings a spouse or a child, the annualised rural filings are ~4,244 investors against 2,646 visas, already 1.6× oversubscribed. Every real value makes it worse and none makes it better, so the direction is settled and only the severity is unknown.

  • Expect yes — the October bulletin should still read current for rural.   • The retrogression is real; it arrives later. It lands when adjudication catches up with the filing wave, which points at FY2027 or FY2028.

Form I-956F — which projects USCIS has actually approved

⚠️ Why you care: every sponsor says “approved” and the word covers four positions. Only some let a September petition ever be approved.

Every sponsor on this board says “approved”. The word covers four different positions, and the difference decides whether an I-526E filed this month can ever be approved — a petition cannot be approved until the project’s I-956F is. USCIS can now deny your petition without ever asking you a question

  • 🔴 Policy Alert PA-2026-05, effective 5 August 2026, lets USCIS deny an I-526E or I-829 with no RFE and no NOID. It applies to petitions “pending or filed on or after” that date, so it already governs anything filed this month.
  • What changes for you: a curable paperwork gap that used to draw a request for evidence can now end the petition outright. The filing has to be complete on the day it is sent, not completable later.
  • 🔴 Denials are running at 24.0% of completed I-526E petitions in FY2026 Q3 — 217 of 904, against 19.3% across Q1–Q2. Three quarters of escalation, read off the agency’s own workbook.
  • ⚠️ The rural-specific denial rate is not published. USCIS breaks the set-asides out for receipts only, so nobody outside the agency can say whether rural runs better or worse than 24%.
  • 🔴 Nobody has sued over the 18 September rule, so plan for that date to hold. Eleven federal dockets mention public charge since the rule was published; every one is an individual case, and none seeks to vacate or stay the rule itself.
  • ⚠️ Stated as a dated negative, because a zero needs its limits. The federal docket mirror holds only what someone has bought or uploaded, so a suit filed days ago may not show — but eight days is not enough to file, brief and decide one.
  • The comparison that makes it meaningful: the 2019 public-charge rule drew state and charity suits within days. This one has drawn none in seven weeks.

The money leaves before USCIS looks at the petition

  • On all five EB5AN funds escrow releases on filing, not on approval. The $800,000 and the $80,000 administration fee are gone from your control the day the petition is receipted, and the administration fee is forfeited in full on a source-of-funds denial — which is the denial that depends on your own file rather than on any sponsor.
  • Board-wide count, and it is unanimous: 0 of the 22 offerings condition release on conditional permanent residence. The release trigger has actually been read and quoted in 4 of the 22 — 002, 003, 008 and 015 — and all four release on filing. ⚠️ The other 18 are not stated in the file, which is not the same as not existing: the trigger may sit in a PPM nobody has produced, so read 17 as unexamined rather than as filing-based. ⚠️ One of the four is a trap worth naming, because a keyword read of it gives the wrong answer. 008’s Escrow Agreement §4 conditions release on the Form I-956 being approved, the I-956F having “been filed”, and “the I-526E Petition for a Subscriber has been filed with USCIS”.
  • The word approved attaches to the I-956 — the regional centre’s own designation — not to your petition. Scanning for “approv” near “escrow” reads that as approval-based release.
  • It is not. It is filing. 003 is the one that shows the mechanism: its Escrow Agreement carries two competing release paths — an approval-based one (“a Subscriber’s I-526E Petition has been approved by USCIS, the Escrow Agent shall disburse…”) and a filing-based one introduced by “Notwithstanding anything to the contrary”, under a PPM heading that says it outright: “Release of Subscription Amount from Escrow Prior to I-526E Petition Approval”.
  • The filing path controls. And releasing early destroys a remedy: “in the event that the denied Subscriber’s Subscription Proceeds were released from the Escrow Account, no such substitution shall be made” (Escrow §4(e)), with refunds “without deduction or payment of interest”.
  • Now the part that reframes all of it: the law would allow the opposite, and nobody sells it. Proposed 8 CFR 204.401 would codify the outer limit — “Actively in the process of investing means an actual commitment to invest… no later than the date on which the investor obtains conditional permanent resident status. Such capital may be held in escrow for release to the new commercial enterprise no later than the date on which the investor obtains conditional permanent resident status.” So escrow may lawfully run all the way to the green card.
  • Every offering here uses the earliest permissible moment; the rule permits the latest. Where a set-aside category is retrogressed, the gap between those two dates is the whole wait for a visa number — years in which capital could lawfully have stayed recoverable and does not. ⚠️ And the same document says two different things. The preamble repeatedly describes escrow as running “pending approval of his or her EB-5 immigrant visa petition” and speaks of “after the approval… and the investor’s capital is released from escrow”, while the regulatory text sets the bound at conditional permanent residence — which in a retrogressed category arrives years later.
  • Two different dates in one rulemaking, and the codified text is the one that would bind. A sponsor asked for escrow-to-CPR can point at the preamble to refuse; the answer is the proposed definition.

The ten jobs are a number from a model — and since 2022 there is a hard deadline on them

  • Asked plainly: if the jobs have not been created, is it enough that someone runs the formula? For a regional-centre investor, substantially yes. The ten jobs can be output from an economic model, and nobody ever has to name ten employees.
  • USCIS’s own Form I-829 says so on its face: Part 8, Item 2.a asks for the “Number of Full-Time Economically Direct, Indirect and Induced Jobs Created” — “economically direct” is a modelling term, not a payroll term — and Item 5 asks, if ten jobs “have not yet been created”, for the number “expected to be created within a reasonable time”.
  • The government printed a box for jobs that do not exist yet. That is the single biggest reason regional-centre EB-5 is easier than direct EB-5, and it is genuine.
  • But three things break it, and they are where money is actually lost. First, the money has to have been spent. The model is accepted; its inputs are audited.
  • Construction expenditure that never happened produces no modelled jobs, so a stalled project does not simply create jobs more slowly — it stops creating them.
  • Second, and this is the one no marketing deck mentions: there is now a hard outer deadline. The jobs must exist by the third anniversary of the conditional green card, or conditional status terminates by operation of statute — INA 216A(c)(3)(B)(ii)(II)(bb), added by the 2022 reform.
  • The old open-ended “reasonable time” is gone for anyone filing today. Third, since 15 March 2024 a physical site visit is a statutory precondition to removing conditions, under INA 216A(c)(1)(C).
  • Someone from the government goes and looks.
  • Put those together and the risk on this board is not that a model is wrong. It is that spending stops while the clock keeps running — which is precisely the shape of the one documented failure involving EB5AN.
  • And there is a fourth thing, which is the one an actual adjudication turned on: the jobs are a pool, and investors are paid out of it in order. Under 8 CFR 204.6(g)(2) jobs already allocated to earlier investors are subtracted before yours are counted. ⚠️ And read the indirect-jobs cap correctly, because the natural reading is the wrong one. The statute caps the share of the whole ten-job requirement that indirect jobs may fill — not the share of the construction-derived jobs. 8 U.S.C. 1153(b)(5)(E)(iv)(I): indirect jobs may satisfy “only up to 90 percent of the requirement under subparagraph (A)(ii)”.
  • And (E)(iv)(II): “If the jobs estimated to be created are created by construction activity lasting less than 2 years”, that ceiling drops to “only up to 75 percent of the requirement under subparagraph (A)(ii)”.
  • So a short build does not merely discount its own construction jobs — it tightens the cap on the entire requirement, and it cannot be worked around by pointing at jobs from somewhere else. In practice: at 90% at least one job in ten must be direct; at 75% at least two and a half in ten must be, and proposed 8 CFR 204.421(b) would separately require “at least one job per regional center investor” to be a direct job.
  • ✔ Applied to the finalists that floor is nowhere near binding, worth stating rather than leaving implied: 022 models 1,251.9 direct against the 150 its maximum needs (8.3×), 014 2,058.4 against 271 (7.6×), and charging all 626.7 of 014’s revenue jobs to the direct column still leaves 1,431.7, 5.3×. Both builds run past two years, so both take the 90% cap.
  • A 2011 decision on a regional-centre dairy shows the arithmetic in the government’s own words: 14 direct jobs at the approved 2.66 multiplier gave 37.24, minus 13 filled by non-qualifying employees gave 24.24, minus “the 20 jobs that have been allocated” to two investors who had already removed conditions, so “we could not allocate more than four of the indirect jobs to the petitioner”.
  • Order: petition denied. A project 20% short left the last investor 58% short. ⚠️ And the short-build penalty is TWO separate rules, not one. Reading it as one understates the arithmetic. (E)(iv)(II) tightens the cap on the whole requirement from 90% to 75%, as above.
  • ⚠️ Two penalties compound, and they sit in different subparagraphs. A build lasting under two years multiplies its direct construction jobs by the fraction of the two years it runs — an 18-month build scores 0.75 — and (E)(iv)(II) separately caps indirect at 75%. The shrinking clause is 8 U.S.C. 1153(b)(5)(E)(v)(II)(cc), headed “Construction activity jobs”.
  • 🔴 So if the schedule slips, the approved application does not automatically still protect you. The binding-effect clause, 8 U.S.C. 1153(b)(5)(F)(ii), is generous on its face — approval “shall be binding… by immigrants investing in the same offering… and of petitions by the same immigrants filed under section 1186b” — and 1186b is removal of conditions, so the protection is written to reach the I-829, years later.
  • And this is not just the statute read at a distance: all 10 approved I-956Fs on this board reproduce that sentence VERBATIM in USCIS’s own words, § 216A limb included — agency documents in hand, not a reading. It is also identical in all ten, so it cannot tell one project from another.
  • But it is expressly conditional: “unless… (III) there has been a material change that affects eligibility” (the other four exits are fraud, public-safety or national-security concerns, undisclosed evidence, and a material mistake of law or fact).
  • A duration change affects eligibility by both of the routes just described — it moves the direct-job multiplier and it moves the cap — so a build that slips across 24 months is not a scheduling detail, it is the exception that switches the binding effect off. ⚠️ The cure exists, and it belongs to the sponsor, not to the investor. (F)(iii)(I) lets a centre seek an amendment “not later than 30 days after any such changes”; (F)(iii)(II) then says that on approval of a timely filed amendment the changes “may be incorporated into and considered in determining program eligibility” for pending petitions.
  • May, not shall. Proposed 8 CFR 204.424(c)(5) would codify the same 30-day duty for changes “materially impacting the credibility or viability of such plans that could adversely impact eligibility for associated investors”.
  • And a separate clock applies to the centre itself: (E)(vi)(I)(aa) requires notice “not later than 120 days before the implementation of significant proposed changes to its organizational structure, ownership, or administration”.
  • So the investor carries the eligibility risk while only the sponsor can file the fix, inside 30 days, and even then incorporation is discretionary. How exposed is this board? A duration is stated in 11 of the 22 offerings and 10 state none; 022 has not been classified on this axis. Of those that do, the files put 001 at ~9–12 months (its own scorecard flags the double penalty 🔴), 016 at 29 months, 014 at ~28, 003 at ~60 and 008 at 81.
  • Two sit on the line itself: 004 is contested inside its own file — 27 months on the deck’s March 2026 start, 20 months if the genuine start is October 2026, and the file records that both readings cannot hold; and 006 is at exactly 24 months (June 2026 to June 2028), scored as clearing S2 with the note “Exactly 24 months, USCIS accepted ‘2 years’”.
  • Exactly 24 is zero headroom: a one-day slip crosses into the double penalty, and whether the approved application still shields the petition then depends on a sponsor filing an amendment within 30 days. ⚠️ Read the 10 with no stated duration as unexamined, not as safe. 🔴 And the ordering rule is the single most important sentence on this page for a filer going in on the last day. The current regulation supplies no ordering rule at all — it defers to “any reasonable agreement” among the investors, so an agreement written pro rata spreads a shortfall instead of concentrating it.
  • The proposed rule supplies a default, and the default is the worst possible one for him: “If no such agreement exists, USCIS will allocate job creation based on the filing date of the EB-5 immigrant visa petition” (proposed 8 CFR 204.407(e)(2)).
  • Filing on 17 September 2026 puts him behind every earlier investor in the same offering, unless the offering documents contain an allocation agreement that says otherwise. And the proposed rule spells out what being behind actually looks like, which is not a denial. Where established job creation is “less than the amount needed to satisfy the job creation requirement for all associated regional center investors”, USCIS allocates by that filing-date rule and issues a notice of partial approval — proposed 8 CFR 204.423(b)(3)(ii).
  • The investors inside the allocation are approved; the ones outside it are left to a further amendment by the regional centre and to the one-year extension at proposed 216.6(g)(3), against the three-year statutory job deadline.
  • So a shortfall does not fail the project. It sorts the investors, and the sort key is the date on the receipt. So the question is not “how many jobs per investor”.
  • It is four numbers, and the third one decides the other three: total modelled jobs; the maximum investor count the offering permits; what the allocation clause actually says; and how many investors have already been allocated theirs. On this board only the first is ever published.
  • One thing runs the other way, and it is worth knowing. The methodology approved at I-526E is locked in for the I-829 — the appeal body, applying Chang v. United States, 327 F.3d 911 (9th Cir. 2003), held the director “erred in revisiting the appropriateness of the multiplier” and that the investor “should be able to rely on” it.
  • So the modelling risk is fixed at the front end, which is exactly why the pool-and-position questions have to be asked before signing rather than after.
  • Two more requirements that are easy to miss because they are stated in different places. Proposed 8 CFR 204.407(e)(3) sets the floor at “at least ten (10) qualifying employees per regional center investor” — ten times the investor count and nothing more.
  • And proposed 204.421(b) adds a separate one: “At least one job per regional center investor must be a direct job”, so a project whose whole count is indirect and induced does not qualify however large the model output. The words buffer, cushion, excess job and surplus appear zero times in the 127-page proposed rule — but DHS does name the idea in its own vocabulary, contemplating “additional jobs that are unnecessary for investor eligibility” at proposed 204.423(c)(5).
  • So the 20–30% cushion is a market convention with no numeric anchor in the rules, not a concept the rules have never heard of.

The one protection every offering advertises, and no public record can verify for any of them

  • Every sponsor in this market markets a money-back guarantee on I-526E approval, and none of them names the guarantor. A competitor’s marketing, quoted in a court exhibit, is the clean example: “Investor Protections: Behring offers a money-back guaranty on I-526E approval, further minimizing risk for EB-5 participants.” Read that sentence for who is legally bound and it says nothing — “Behring offers” is a brand, not an entity with a balance sheet.
  • The distinction is not pedantry. It is the whole value of the guarantee. In the single EB-5 deal anywhere whose executed loan agreement can be read in public, the job-creation guaranty and the I-526E refund guaranty both run from the borrower — the contract literally styles the developer as “Job Creation Guarantor” and “I-526E Approval Refund Guarantor”.
  • So the protection is written by the party whose failure is the event it insures against, and it is worth nothing at the only moment it is needed.

Searching the whole federal court record for those exact contract terms returns five documents and two documents, and every one is that same bankruptcy — against a positive control that returned a full page for “I-526E” and a negative control that returned nothing.

Method

Every document reachable from any sponsor was collected and made machine-readable — 148 source documents in all, including roughly 3,000 pages of scans put through optical character recognition so they could be searched rather than skimmed. Marketing decks, offering memoranda, loan agreements, partnership agreements, guaranties, escrow agreements, economic reports and government approval notices were read against each other and against records held by parties with no interest in the outcome: the immigration agency’s own approval notices and its two published regional-centre lists, securities filings read directly from the regulator, federal court records, county land and property records, and local reporting.

Three rules were applied throughout. A confidence label belongs to the quotation, never to the conclusion drawn from it — a verbatim quote with a wrong inference on top is the failure that survives review, because the evidence underneath it is real. Every stated absence carries a control that could have failed; an uncontrolled “not found” is a guess wearing a credential it did not earn. And where a sponsor’s own documents disagree with each other, that disagreement is itself the finding, recorded with both figures and where each appears.

Every finding was then put through an adversarial pass whose default posture was refutation, in both directions — because wrongly clearing a bad project and wrongly condemning a sound one cost the same, and with a filing deadline close there is no time to recover from either.
